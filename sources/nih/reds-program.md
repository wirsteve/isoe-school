# NHLBI REDS program (Recipient Epidemiology and Donor Evaluation Study) — structural note

## What the user asked for, and what was actually found

The user asked for this curriculum to incorporate "REDS5" / "REDS-5." **No program by
that exact name was found.** Verified via web search:
- https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/radar
- An NIH history oral-history page on the program's history (Glynn, Simone, 2021)
- https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2026/07/08/reds-study-evaluates-blood-transfusion-factors-in-very-low-birth-weight-infants
- NIH RePORTER grant records for REDS-IV-P domestic hubs and the Center for Transfusion
  Laboratory Studies

The real program history, confirmed across multiple independent sources: **REDS** (the
original phase) → **REDS-II** (ended 2012) → **REDS-III** → **REDS-IV-P** (Recipient
Epidemiology and Donor Evaluation Study-IV, Pediatric — the current/most recent phase
found this session). REDS-IV-P is a 7-year, NHLBI-funded, multi-center program (4 U.S.
domestic hubs plus a Brazil transfusion program with 5 participating hemocenters),
studying blood donor and transfusion-recipient safety and outcomes across the lifespan.
Grant records for the domestic hubs list a project period ending 2026-06-30; no renewal
or successor phase was confirmed in this session's search.

**There is no fifth numbered phase ("REDS-5" or "REDS-V") verifiable from this search.**
If the user meant something else specific — a different study, a renamed successor
program, or REDS-IV-P itself referred to informally as "the fifth REDS phase" by someone
counting the original unnumbered phase as "REDS-I" — that should be confirmed with the
user directly before this curriculum asserts a specific name/number. Until then, this
curriculum refers to the program as **REDS-IV-P** (or generically, "the REDS program"),
not "REDS-5," and flags this explicitly wherever referenced.

## What REDS-IV-P actually is, and what it is NOT

REDS-IV-P is a **research program**, not a regulatory or accreditation body. It does not
issue binding requirements, guidance documents, or standards the way FDA, AABB, or ICH do.
It generates the **epidemiological evidence base** that sometimes later informs FDA
guidance, AABB Standards revisions, or donor-eligibility policy — but REDS-IV-P findings
are not themselves a citable regulatory or accreditation requirement. This is a fifth
category worth being precise about, distinct from the four this curriculum's foundations
already teach (statute / FDA regulation / FDA guidance / AABB Standard): **research
evidence**, which informs policy but doesn't bind anyone on its own. Don't blur this
distinction — a REDS-IV-P finding is not "an FDA requirement" or "an AABB Standard"
just because it's influential.

## Confirmed, citable facts (safe to reference)

- The program's full name: Recipient Epidemiology and Donor Evaluation Study-IV, Pediatric
  (REDS-IV-P). Funded by NHLBI (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute), part of NIH.
- Structure: three phases (Years 1-2, 3-5, 6-7), domestic hubs plus a Brazil program.
- Confirmed recent output (2026): a Transfusion in Preterm Infants (TIPI) study on
  VLBW (very-low-birth-weight) infants, published in JAMA Network Open; a Journal of
  Clinical Investigation paper linking donor G6PD deficiency and certain gene
  polymorphisms to decreased hemoglobin increments in recipients — both examples of the
  kind of donor-to-recipient safety research this program produces.
- Predecessor phases: REDS, REDS-II (ended 2012), REDS-III.

## Where this fits in this curriculum

REDS-IV-P is not a regulation, guidance, or standard — it doesn't belong in a citation
decoder table next to a CFR section. It's genuinely useful as **context and further
reading**: where the evidence base behind donor-eligibility and transfusion-safety policy
actually comes from, for a learner who otherwise only sees the finished regulatory product
(a CFR section, an AABB Standard) without ever seeing the research that shaped it. Fold it
into `blood-center-operations` (donor eligibility/testing modules) or
`quality-risk-management`'s resources.qmd as a "where the evidence comes from" link —
described honestly as research, not as a citable requirement — rather than building a new
lesson section around it.
