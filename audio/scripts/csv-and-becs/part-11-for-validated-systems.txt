This is the ISOE School audio briefing for Part Eleven for Validated Systems, from the Computerized System Validation and becks course. It matters to you because the same vendor access request that looks like a security question to I T is also a records-control question to the F D A, and you're the one who has to answer both.

Here's the story. Lakeshore Blood Center is upgrading its becks, its blood establishment computer software, under a project called V A L twelve-oh-three. Buried in a statement of work addendum, the vendor's consultant asks for persistent remote access to the central becks application server after go-live. Standing credentials, no stated expiration, justified as faster issue resolution.

You've seen this before. This vendor's remote-support platform was once the subject of a security incident, though nothing becks-specific was compromised. And a vendor risk assessment, V R A oh-two-nineteen, found that the vendor's claim of always notifying customers before a change didn't survive a check against the deviation log. One change arrived with no notice at all.

The second thread is quieter. The requirements specification for V A L twelve-oh-three still has no row saying becks refuses to release a component before all required results are recorded, or refuses a release from a role that isn't authorized. Those behaviors are almost certainly true. Nobody has written a test that tries to make becks fail at either one.

Here's the first idea. Part Eleven only reaches records that some other regulation already requires you to keep. It sets the conditions under which an electronic record is as trustworthy as paper. The underlying rule, like twenty-one C F R six-oh-six point one-sixty or twenty-one C F R two-eleven point six-eight, is called a predicate rule. Part Eleven rides on top of it and never creates a new recordkeeping duty.

Second. Closed versus open systems turn on who controls access, not on whether an outsider can connect. Under twenty-one C F R eleven point three, a closed system is one where access is controlled by the people responsible for the records' content. An open system is one where it isn't. If Lakeshore grants the consultant a defined window, logs the session, and can revoke it alone, becks stays closed, even with a vendor at the keyboard. Standing credentials with no logging or revocation drift toward open, and toward the heavier obligations of eleven point thirty, which adds encryption and digital-signature measures as necessary. So the honest answer is, it depends on how the access is built.

If you think in your own vocabulary, this is privileged access management. Time-bound checkouts, logged sessions, no standing credentials. And the F D A's own guidance recommends a remote access log with the date, time, reason, and name of the person connecting. It's a recommendation, not a requirement, but it's exactly the evidence that shows who controlled the access.

Third. The F D A doesn't enforce every word of Part Eleven equally. Its two-thousand-three scope and application guidance says it will exercise enforcement discretion on validation and audit trails, while naming controls it still intends to enforce. Among them are limiting system access, operational system checks, authority checks, device checks, and training.

That lands on three paragraphs of twenty-one C F R eleven point ten. Paragraph f requires operational checks that enforce permitted sequencing. That's the quarantine release gate in software. Paragraph g requires authority checks, so only authorized people can perform the operation at hand. And paragraph eye requires that people who use the system have the education, training, and experience for their tasks. The F D A's becks guidance reaches training from a separate direction, in section three, part G.

The point of all this is that a policy saying only authorized staff release components is not proof. A test case that tries to make becks release early, or lets the wrong role release, and records that becks refused, is proof.

So on Monday, do three things. Before you sign off on the remote access, ask who grants it, who logs it, and who can revoke it. If any answer is, the vendor decides, that's open-system drift happening in real time. Require the remote access log for this vendor, given that earlier risk assessment.

Second, send the V A L twelve-oh-three test plan back until it has real test cases for paragraphs eff and gee, flagged in the requirements specification now, before test design locks in.

Third, ask for training records for the specific people operating the upgrade. Dated, individual, and tied to the procedures they perform. A general statement that staff are trained won't hold up.

An inspector will ask for exactly those three things. They won't ask whether you have a Part Eleven policy.

Now the trap. Someone will say eleven point ten, paragraph ay, is the F D A's main enforcement hook for becks validation. Not quite. That paragraph is on the discretion list, and the guidance leans on the predicate rules, two-eleven point six-eight and Part six-oh-six, to require your validation. The opposite trap is just as bad. Remote access does not automatically make becks open. Neither the reflex answer nor the relaxed one looks at who actually controls the access.
