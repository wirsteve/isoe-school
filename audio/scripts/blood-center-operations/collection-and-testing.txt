This is the ISOE School audio briefing for Collection and testing, from the Blood Center Operations course. It matters to you because the control that keeps a bad unit away from a patient isn't a person or a procedure. It's a status in a system, and you own the systems.

Here's the story. Tuesday morning, at a Lakeshore Blood Center mobile site, a phlebotomist draws a whole blood unit. Before the needle goes in, the donor, the collection bag, and a set of small sample tubes are all tied together under one number. That number will follow the donation through every record it ever generates.

By Tuesday afternoon, the unit is already at Lakeshore's processing lab. It's been spun and split into three products. Red cells go into a refrigerator. Plasma goes into a freezer. Platelets go onto a room-temperature agitator in another part of the building. Meanwhile the sample tubes are in the testing lab, running the infectious-disease panel and the blood-typing work.

Wednesday morning, one of those tests comes back reactive. Now Lakeshore has three products in three pieces of equipment, and the only thing connecting them is that Tuesday number. All three must be found and held, and the clock started when the result posted, not when someone noticed.

Here's the first idea. Processing and testing run in parallel, not in sequence. Components have short shelf lives, so separation starts almost immediately, while the tubes are still in the lab. Fully processed products sitting in storage do not mean testing is done. That's normal, not a shortcut.

Second. The control is a gate, not a sequencing rule. If you work in change management, you already know this. You build, test, and stage in parallel, but nothing crosses into production until the gate opens. Quarantine is a release gate. In Lakeshore's blood establishment computer software, which most of this course calls becks, every product from a donation carries a quarantine status. It's checked before release, shipping, or use, wherever the product physically sits.

Third. A reactive result isn't one obligation. It's at least three. The products have to be pulled and quarantined or destroyed. The donor is deferred. And the donor is notified. Depending on which test was reactive, there may also be supplemental testing and a lookback into that donor's earlier donations. A failed gate here carries consequences a failed build never does.

Now the rule, in plain words. Start with twenty-one C F R six-oh-six point one-sixty, paragraph see. It requires a donor number that relates the unit, the donor's medical record, every component made from that unit, and all the records of their disposition. One number, and everything traces back to it. That's how the system finds the plasma and the red cells the moment the platelets are linked to a reactive donation.

Next, twenty-one C F R six-ten point four-oh, paragraph gee. Blood can be released or shipped before testing is complete only in a documented medical emergency, or for further manufacturing as approved in writing by the F D A. That's written as an exception, which means the default is wait. Lakeshore's platelets sit in quarantine on Wednesday morning because the regulation makes waiting the rule.

And paragraph aitch, one, of the same section. You must not ship or use blood or blood components with a reactive screening test, with narrow exceptions. Notice it says components, plural. It reaches the platelets in the agitator and the plasma in the freezer exactly as much as it reaches the unit still in the lab.

So what do you do Monday? First, ask how quarantine status spreads the moment a result posts. If the testing lab's system flags the unit right away but an overnight batch job flags the components elsewhere, there's a real window where a product could ship. Ask for the actual latency, not the intended design.

Second, when your Quality Analyst brings you a report on a reactive result, check whether it covers the donor side. Deferral, notification, and any lookback, not just the product disposition.

Third, if the processing lab's inventory and the testing lab's laboratory information system can disagree about which products trace to a donation number, treat that as a data integrity gap. The donation number is the single source of truth for what a result applies to.

Here's what an F D A investigator will ask for. The donation record showing the single number and every product that traces to it. A timestamped quarantine action on all three products, not only the one in the lab. Any gap in those timestamps, however small, is where a unit could have shipped. And the donor notification, plus the lookback record if one applies.

Now the trap to avoid. It's the answer that sounds like, the technician who saw the result would have called processing. That describes a hope, not a control. The control is a system-wide status check keyed to the donation number. A related trap is thinking a negative on one test clears the donation. Release requires every required test in the panel to be complete and passing, so a negative on one infection says nothing about the others.

One more, and it's subtle. If a sample tube is mislabeled but the bag is labeled correctly, that is not a paperwork issue. The tube is what gives the test result its meaning, so a tube identity error is a testing-system failure.
